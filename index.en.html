The Dark Arc +3.5 °C · 18%

Climate-society foresight · 2025 → 2100 · variant VI

The dark arc

The same physical world as before — forcing from Greenland's melt, AMOC crossed around 2035-45 — but this time we follow what societies make of it: water, migration, and a state coming apart.

The throughline

Energy descent (Jancovici) + the metals wall (Bihouix) → the state can no longer hold its services → water wars and internal migration. Fragility optimised for performance (Hamant) fails in cascade. In the background, a constrained modernity: AI and machines don't disappear — they shrink, rationed, repaired, unevenly guarded.

And a reversal: La Faye, with its "normal" winters, becomes land people flee; Folligny, cooler and still recharging its water table each winter, a refuge under pressure. Climatic normality doesn't save you without water.

A foresight exercise, not a forecast. The physical figures reuse the "Greenland forcing" variant already built; at fine scales (village level), every narrative is illustration anchored in real geography, not data. This variant deliberately explores a pessimistic branch, not the most probable one. The thermal framework used (+3.5 °C in 2100, no plateau) matches the central value of CMIP7's "High" scenario published in April 2026 (van Vuuren et al.), which now rules out SSP5-8.5 as implausible: the branch occupies the plausible ceiling of current science, not the old extreme tail. The annual indicators published in June 2026 (IGCC, Forster et al.) confirm the momentum: human-caused warming of +1.37 °C in 2025, at a record pace of +0.27 °C per decade; Earth's energy imbalance about 40% above the AR6 estimate; the 1.5 °C carbon budget (50%) down to ~130 GtCO₂ in early 2026 — roughly three years of current emissions. This acceleration isn't one team's artefact: an independent re-analysis (Foster & Rahmstorf, Geophysical Research Letters, 2026) puts the acceleration at +75% since 2015 (confidence >98%) once the data are corrected for the El Niño effect — reinforcing, not circumventing, this document's rule that a year-to-year anomaly doesn't make a trajectory. Crossing the AMOC threshold around 2035-45 is a deliberately high-end assumption: the centre of the literature places it later, after 2050 (Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen 2023), and the AR6 judges a collapse before 2100 unlikely (medium confidence) — though van Westen & Rahmstorf argue that CMIP models underestimate freshwater forcing, and the observational constraints published in 2026 point the same way (Portmann et al., Science Advances: ~51% weakening by 2100 even under the median scenario, once salinity biases are corrected; a North Atlantic cold blob confirmed as a signature of the slowdown) — the 2035-45 window used here remains the low bound of the plausible range, which extends out to ~2050. Unless otherwise noted, the summer temperatures cited are record peaks, not seasonal averages, and sea-level rise is relative to the 1995-2014 reference.

Global scale

The great crumbling

Warming doesn't plateau. But the century's real rupture isn't thermal: it's the slow collapse of major states' capacity to protect their populations, and the tilt of humanity toward the poles.

Climate

Every year beats the last — the 1.5 °C carbon budget runs out around 2029, and the Paris Agreement threshold falls without ceremony. Water stress settles in across the Maghreb, the Middle East, South Asia, the American West; monsoon failure in South Asia (~2027). Greenland's melt accelerates — the AMOC is already slowing, quietly.

Observed — 2026South Asia, April-May 2026: the projection takes shape. A pre-monsoon heatwave from mid-April into May — over 46 °C in numerous Indian cities (Akola 46.9 °C, Amravati 46.8 °C); on one day in late April, the fifty hottest cities on the planet were all in India (Carnegie). At least 37 deaths recorded in India, around ten in Pakistan — tolls almost certainly underestimated; agricultural drought over more than a million km², record power demand, rising wet-bulb humidity danger. Attribution (World Weather Attribution, 2026): the episode was made roughly 3 times more likely and ~1 °C hotter by climate change. At ~+1.4 °C such an episode recurs on average every 5 years; at ~+2.6 °C — the order of magnitude of this document's median branch — every 2 to 3 years. On agriculture: an estimated ~8% of wheat yield is lost per additional degree (Carnegie). The "failed monsoon / bread riots ~2027" above is no longer a distant hypothesis — its antechamber is already being measured. An established, dated fact — not a shift in trajectory. One episode doesn't establish the century's climate; it shows that an already-projected stress is starting to materialise.
Society

The world still functions. But the bread riots in India and Pakistan (~2027) and the Egypt–Ethiopia standoff over the Nile (~2029), defused by fragile mediation, are a preview. Climate displacement swells with no institutional name yet.

2050 — 2070

The cascade

world ≈ +3.0 °Csea +0.45 mwater ▮▮▮▮
Climate

Arid bands and, in places, tropics close to uninhabitable in summer. Reefs collapsed, fisheries in decline.

Society

The global system, optimised for performance rather than robustness (Hamant's diagnosis), fails in cascade. Couplings between food↔transport↔energy↔finance propagate failure with no shock absorber: systemic interdependence, never addressed, turns against us (Keller). Failed states multiply across the hot belt. Migration reorganises the species toward the poles. Famine and epidemics hit health systems already in retreat. Resource nationalism becomes the grammar of international relations.

Technology

The great technical divergence: enclaves keep data centres, robots and drones — at the cost of a growing share of their energy. Everywhere else, maintenance replaces renewal: the age of stock supersedes the age of flow (Bihouix).

Continental scale

Two Europes

The South empties out and catches fire; the Atlantic North becomes an overwhelmed fortress refuge. The Union, as we knew it, doesn't survive the combination of migration, energy, and finance.

Observed — 2026Two very early heatwaves in Western Europe within four weeks. Late May then late June 2026, Western Europe — France foremost — absorbs two off-the-charts episodes. On June 24, France records the hottest day ever measured on its soil and holds the planet's strongest thermal anomaly (up to +20 °C above normals on its Atlantic coast); peaks of 42-44 °C, 72 départements under red alert. Météo-France: these episodes are becoming earlier and more intense. Summer 2026 — the fire season synchronises. As of August 1, EFFIS counted, as of July 29, ~1,200 fires across the Union against a long-term average of 569 — more than double — burning at least 250,000 ha (published figures diverge, up to 457,000 ha). Reminder: 2025 had already been the Union's worst season since EFFIS record-keeping began in 2006, with over 1.08 million hectares and national records in Germany, Spain, Cyprus and Slovakia; France contributed over 119,000 ha of that, including the 42,000 ha in Gironde (~220,000 people evacuated, fire contained on August 1). The new element isn't the fire itself, it's the synchronisation of fire conditions across the continent: when everything becomes combustible at the same time, mutual aid becomes the scarce resource — the rescEU mechanism pools 777 firefighters from 14 countries. Guardrail — a hot spring doesn't predict the summer, still less the century (cf. 1953, 2001; cool interludes remain possible, e.g. 2023). NOAA also moved its status on June 11, 2026 from El Niño Watch to El Niño Advisory (the WMO had announced the event on June 2), with a possible "Super El Niño" — probability now around 63% of reaching "very strong" level, with a peak expected between November 2026 and January 2027 and near-certain persistence into winter. In other words: the next twelve months' records will partly be ENSO-driven, and should be read as such — this rule is set out before the fact, not after: part of the year's anomaly is thus known, documented interannual variability — one more reason not to read these records as proof of trajectory. Established facts that recalibrate the short term, not proof of a 2100 trajectory.

National scale

The state that withdraws

Water becomes the master variable. Exhausted by disasters, the state triages: it protects metropolises and strategic infrastructure, and lets the periphery go. France recomposes around the Atlantic North-West.

Early signals — June 2026. The PwC study "Steering Through Uncertainty" (70+ interviews with local government directors) documents, in the present, the early signs of this arc: public deficit at 5.1% (2025), debt > €3,400bn; local-authority climate adaptation costed at €21bn by 2030 (IGF), largely unfunded; France already Europe's most exposed country to natural disasters — 4 very severe events a year since 2001, against 1 a year between 1950 and 2000; insurability problems for public infrastructure; 1 in 2 inter-municipal bodies at land-use saturation; −24% births in 2025 versus 2010. Local government directors speak of "permanent crisis management" and a mandate shifted "from investment to trade-offs": the vocabulary of the 2049 triage is already being written, in outline. At the same time, science is measuring its own weakening: the IGCC 2025 report (Forster et al., published June 11, 2026) documents an underfunded global climate observation system — satellite missions at risk, the ESA TRUTHS mission cancelled, in-situ networks thinning, no guaranteed continuity for the GCOS programme. The eyes are closing just as everything accelerates.
Observed — 2026Two exceptional heatwaves in four weeks — both very early. May (21-30): on May 26, the hottest May day ever measured in France (24.8 °C national average); 37.8 °C at Angoulême-La Couronne, an absolute May record for mainland France; the first-ever May heatwave alert — but these are monthly records, not absolute ones (Météo-France). June: June 24 becomes the hottest day ever measured in France (29.9 °C average), the night of June 22-23 the hottest since 1947 (21.6 °C), 72 départements under red alert (a record), France showing the planet's strongest anomaly (up to +20 °C in the West). Agroclimatologist Serge Zaka counts ~470 records, including 118 absolute records, peaks of 42-44 °C — "and we're only in June." Météo-France: episodes increasingly early and intense, a frequency that could be multiplied tenfold by 2100. July: the water dimension. The 2026 anchor isn't only thermal. On July 30, agroclimatologist Serge Zaka documents the most intense July drought ever observed in France — ahead of 1976, 2003 and 2022 — with the Poitou-Burgundy band hit hardest. At the same time, Normandy (Manche, Calvados, Orne, Eure) moves to crisis level with restrictions reaching drinking water: summer water scarcity no longer spares the wet coast. Heat and drought stop being two separate stories. The conflict between nuclear-fleet cooling and river flow — known in France since 2003, revisited in 2018, 2019 and 2022 — changes scale elsewhere in Europe: the Danube falls to −108 cm east of Croatia, forcing Hungary and Romania to cut nuclear output simultaneously. The mechanism isn't new; its cross-border simultaneity, in 2026, is. Guardrail — a scorching spring and early summer predict neither the summer nor the century: there was 1953, 2001, and cool interludes remain possible (Météo-France points to 2023). These facts recalibrate the short-term floor and show already-projected stresses arriving; they don't prove a 2100 trajectory.

Manche département (50)

A refuge under pressure

Relatively spared — cool Atlantic, winters still wet — the département becomes an overwhelmed haven for displaced people from the South, while absorbing storms, coastal retreat, and winter cold snaps.

Observed — 2026June 2026: "the Norman shield no longer works" (Météo-France). The Manche coast, long a thermal buffer, has been caught up: Cherbourg 37 °C (absolute record, +4 °C above the previous one), Bernières-sur-Mer 40.3 °C, Dieppe 37 °C; inland in the département, Granville 39 °C and Pontorson ~41 °C (already in May, Gouville-sur-Mer 35.3 °C). The idea of a coast durably spared by the cool Atlantic is softened: maritime moderation still cushions, but no longer protects. A record is a floor — the coast will no longer durably drop below ~38 °C at summer peak, hence the recalibrated starting point for the Folligny/Manche projections. July 2026: water, not just heat. On July 10, the prefecture places the département under orange heatwave alert and issues an order the same day restricting forestry work and activities; fifteen of the forty fireworks displays planned for July 11-15 are cancelled. This combination — heat, drought, fire risk — was until now foreign to this coastline. On July 15, two hydrographic sectors of the département — the North Cotentin and the Sienne-Soulles basin — are placed at maximum crisis level: more than 110 municipalities under a total watering ban, irrigation banned down to drip systems. Already by late June, the Saint-Lô Agglo reservoirs' autonomy had fallen to twelve hours after a 50% rise in consumption, drinking water at risk in nine municipalities. The Norman shield isn't only giving way on temperature: it's giving way on the resource too. An established, dated fact. Recalibrates the short-term floor; changes neither the 2100 ranking of the branches (fixed by CMIP7) nor the AMOC reasoning.

Germany · North Rhine-Westphalia

The artery and the lock

France is read through its aquifers; Germany is read through a river. The Rhine is not a landscape there: it is the circulatory system of a heavy industry that never had a plan B. In this branch the lock does not give way at once — it closes by one more summer every decade.

Observed — 2026The summer of 2026 moved the floor, and it did so two months too early. On 30 July the Rhine at Lobith, five kilometres downstream of Emmerich, stood at 6.47 m NAP — below the August 2022 record, in a series opened in 1866. On 6 August discharge there fell to 655 m³/s, the lowest figure ever measured at that point in the year since 1901; the KNMI writes that breaking the all-time low of 620 m³/s (November 1947) "certainly cannot be ruled out". Upstream, the chain of German gauges gives way together: Kaub 5 cm on 17 August against 25 cm on 22 October 2018 — and below the 2018 record every single day from 3 August; Cologne 46 cm against 69; Düsseldorf −2 cm against 23; Duisburg-Ruhrort 126 cm against 153. What matters is not the record, it is the date. 2018 peaked in October, 2022 in late August; 2026 is already below historic floors in early August, with two months of low water ahead of it. The cost is immediate and quantified: convoys carry only 20 to 30 per cent of capacity, freight on the Rotterdam–Karlsruhe route goes from about €45/t in late June to €150-160/t in mid-August, and the Kiel Institute puts the dent in third-quarter German GDP at 0.1-0.2 point. For the record, the 2018 low water — 130 consecutive days below 78 cm at Kaub — cost 0.2 points of third-quarter GDP according to the Bundesbank, some €250 m to BASF, and pushed industrial production down 1.5 per cent year on year. One order of magnitude says everything about the problem: it takes 60 lorries to replace a single barge carrying 1,500 tonnes. Caveat — three record years in eight (2018, 2022, 2026) do not make a statistical trend, and a low-water episode depends first on the weather of the year. What is established is the direction of the long-term hydrological signal (ICPR, report no. 297): fuller winters, leaner summers. The facts of 2026 reset an observed floor; they do not demonstrate the trajectory of the century.

The German thread

Three dependencies overlap on the same river, and the climate puts them in competition at the same time of year. Logistics: chemicals, steel, coal, petroleum products, grain and cocoa go up the Rhine because nothing else can carry those volumes. Cooling: thermal and industrial, it draws from and returns to a river whose temperature rises as its discharge falls. Filling: the exit from Rhineland lignite leaves craters to be flooded, and the plan adopted is to pipe Rhine water into them.

The reference hydrological scenario (ICPR no. 297, 2024, RCP8.5) gives Lobith, for 2031-2060 and against 1981-2010, a mean winter discharge 23 to 44 per cent higher and a summer low flow (NM7Q) down by as much as 36 per cent — worsening further through 2071-2100. In other words: more water when it is not needed, less when everything depends on it. In this branch it is not the river that dries up — it is the industrial calendar that becomes incompatible with the hydrological one.

Cross-scale reading

Three axes, six scales

The same dark arc, reread not by place but by force. Three variables run through the whole foresight exercise and end up summarising it: water, migration, the state.

① Water

From summer restriction to open war. Water stops being a resource and becomes the master variable: it decides who stays and who leaves.

② Migration

A shift toward the poles and cool coastlines — the global South toward the North, the French South and Centre toward the Atlantic North-West. Systemic throughline (Keller)

What produces collapse here isn't the sum of sectoral stresses, but their coupling: water, energy, food, transport, finance and digital systems are so interdependent that a failure propagates in cascade. Germany · North Rhine-Westphalia

The river put on quota

Same river, same physics, same run of records — but a country that this time sees it coming. In this branch Germany does not lose the Rhine: it stops taking it for granted, and pays for that in investment, in rules and in slowness.

Observed — 2026The summer of 2026 moved the floor, and it did so two months too early. On 30 July the Rhine at Lobith, five kilometres downstream of Emmerich, stood at 6.47 m NAP — below the August 2022 record, in a series opened in 1866. On 6 August discharge there fell to 655 m³/s, the lowest ever measured at that point in the year since 1901; the KNMI writes that breaking the all-time low of 620 m³/s (November 1947) "certainly cannot be ruled out". Upstream the chain of German gauges gives way together: Kaub 5 cm on 17 August against 25 cm on 22 October 2018 — and below the 2018 record every single day from 3 August; Cologne 46 cm against 69; Düsseldorf −2 cm against 23; Duisburg-Ruhrort 126 cm against 153. What matters is not the record, it is the date. 2018 peaked in October, 2022 in late August; 2026 is already below historic floors in early August. Convoys carry only 20 to 30 per cent of capacity, freight on the Rotterdam–Karlsruhe route goes from about €45/t in late June to €150-160/t in mid-August, and the Kiel Institute puts the dent in third-quarter German GDP at 0.1-0.2 point. In 2018 — 130 consecutive days below 78 cm at Kaub — the Bundesbank had measured the same effect at 0.2 points, with some €250 m of losses for BASF and industrial production down 1.5 per cent year on year. Caveat — three record years in eight (2018, 2022, 2026) do not make a statistical trend, and a low-water episode depends first on the weather of the year. What is established is the direction of the long-term signal (ICPR, report no. 297): fuller winters, leaner summers. These facts reset an observed floor; they do not demonstrate the trajectory of the century.

The German thread

What separates this branch from the dark one is not hydrological — the river does roughly the same thing. It is institutional. The 2026 signal is this time read: low water stops being treated as an insurable accident and becomes a design constraint. As elsewhere in this branch, insurance withdraws first — cover for low-water surcharges thins out well before the state moves, and it is that withdrawal which forces the arbitration.

The reference scenario (ICPR no. 297, 2024, RCP8.5) gives Lobith, for 2031-2060, a winter discharge 23 to 44 per cent higher and a summer low flow down by as much as 36 per cent. In this branch those figures become an enforceable norm: written priorities of use, a subsidised shallow-draught fleet, funded modal shift to rail, and the pipeline for filling the lignite lakes renegotiated downwards rather than abandoned. The result is neither rescue nor collapse: a river that does less, in a country that has reorganised around that less.

Cross-scale reading

Three axes, six scales — tempered version

The same arc, reread by force rather than by place. Three variables — water, migration, the state — run through the median branch; they close in, but without locking shut.

① Water

From summer restriction to negotiated quota. Water remains the master variable — it decides who adapts and who lightens its load — but open war gives way to arbitration under stress: hard-won treaties, contested quotas, persistent water injustice.

② Migration

A gradual shift toward the poles and cooler coastlines; southern/central France → the Atlantic Northwest. In a France that is contracting (−24% in births from 2025), these flows redistribute more than they add — smoothly, without saturation or desertion. Some places fill out, others lighten: none tips over.

③ The State

From triage to negotiated triage. It ranks and differentiates — metros first, rural areas kept on at a bare minimum — instead of abandoning them. Local self-organization complements the state rather than replacing it; survival is not relegated to the local alone.

Germany · North Rhine-Westphalia

The artery renegotiated

In this branch Germany does with the Rhine what it did with its electricity: it decides early, it pays a great deal, and it keeps the essential. But the old river — the one you could load to capacity twelve months a year — does not come back.

Observed — 2026The summer of 2026 moved the floor, and it did so two months too early. On 30 July the Rhine at Lobith, five kilometres downstream of Emmerich, stood at 6.47 m NAP — below the August 2022 record, in a series opened in 1866. On 6 August discharge there fell to 655 m³/s, the lowest ever measured at that point in the year since 1901; the KNMI writes that breaking the all-time low of 620 m³/s (November 1947) "certainly cannot be ruled out". Kaub fell to 5 cm on 17 August against 25 cm on 22 October 2018, staying below the 2018 record every single day from 3 August; Cologne 46 cm against 69; Düsseldorf −2 cm against 23; Duisburg-Ruhrort 126 cm against 153. Convoys carry only 20 to 30 per cent of capacity, freight on the Rotterdam–Karlsruhe route goes from about €45/t in late June to €150-160/t in mid-August, and the Kiel Institute puts the third-quarter GDP effect at 0.1-0.2 point — the order of magnitude the Bundesbank already measured in 2018. Caveat — three record years in eight (2018, 2022, 2026) do not make a statistical trend, and a low-water episode depends first on the weather of the year. What is established is the direction of the long-term signal (ICPR, report no. 297): fuller winters, leaner summers. These facts reset an observed floor; they do not demonstrate the trajectory of the century.

The German thread

What distinguishes this branch is not foresight — the facts of 2026 are the same everywhere — but that the response arrives while the river still works, rather than afterwards. Modal shift is funded before it is needed; the Emmerich–Oberhausen line is completed in the 2030s instead of dragging on; the Lower Rhine sharing regime is negotiated with the Netherlands as a treaty, not as crisis management. And the pipeline meant to flood the lignite craters is resized at the design stage instead of being defended to the point of absurdity.

The price is real and it is paid up front: taxation, stranded assets, long worksites, and a heavy industry that retracts onto what it can supply without the river. The reference hydrological scenario (ICPR no. 297, RCP8.5 — more severe than this branch's own trajectory) remains the design bound: you build for the worse and get somewhat better. Result: the artery holds, but it no longer carries everything.

Emmerich am Rhein · 46446 · Kreis Kleve · ~31,000 inhabitants

The port that moved first

Emmerich is the last German town on the Rhine: what passes its quays is the balance of everything abstracted upstream. In this branch it uses its position at the end of the chain to see things coming before the others — and turns that into an argument.

Eleven days later, according to the German trade press, the station stopped reporting: the river had fallen below the measuring range of the staff gauge. The last German gauge on the Rhine went silent in the middle of the low-water season — in this branch it is that image which triggers the politics. Cross-scale reading

Three axes, six scales — the bifurcation

The same arc, reread by force rather than by place. Three variables — water, migration, the state — run through the bifurcation; they close in, but without locking shut.

① Water

From summer restriction to negotiated quota. Water remains the master variable — it decides who adapts and who lightens its load — but open war gives way to arbitration under stress: hard-won treaties, contested quotas, persistent water injustice.

② Migration

A gradual shift toward the poles and cooler coastlines; southern/central France → the Atlantic Northwest. In a France that is contracting (−24% in births from 2025), these flows redistribute more than they add — smoothly, without saturation or sudden exodus. Some places fill out, others lighten: none tips over.

③ The State

Of avowed planning. The state reinvests, ranks, and redirects — metros first, rural areas maintained and funded — instead of retreating. Local self-organization complements the state rather than replacing it; survival is not relegated to the local alone.

Systemic throughline (Keller)

The bifurcation treats fragility at its root: not the robustness of each sector, but the organized decoupling of critical systems — redundancy, relocation, unlinking of vital chains. Water↔energy↔food couplings are loosened before the crisis, such that cascades are largely prevented. This choice was paired with work on narratives: dismantling the narrative of abundance, carrying a desirable redirection early, anticipating tipping points that are social as much as biophysical (Arthur Keller).
